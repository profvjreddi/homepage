I got interested in research as an undergraduate. That's not unusual. A lot of the professors I know, and a lot of students now doing PhDs, can point to a stretch of undergrad research that made the work feel real. Someone let them own a piece of a problem. They got stuck. They stayed with it. Something clicked.

This is general advice. It isn't about my lab, or Harvard, or this particular moment in computer science. If you're an undergrad who wants to do research, anywhere, these are the things I wish more students heard before they started trying to join five groups at once.

Most students I talk to can tell me why they want to join a group. Fewer can tell me why they want to do research. Those are different questions.

> You want to work on a question that doesn't have an answer in the back of the book.

That's it. Class is for what's known. Research is what you do when you want to go further than that. The question can be rough: something from a lecture that didn't sit right, a paper you couldn't drop, a system you used that felt broken. "I want to be in a research group" is not a question. It's a request to belong. Belonging comes later, if the work is real.

## You're not joining a club

The email I get most from undergraduates, and the one PhD students in every lab I know also get, is some version of: *I'd like to join the lab and do some work.*

I understand the sentence. It's still the wrong place to start.

A research group isn't a club you join so the name shows up on your CV. Someone agrees to train you on a piece of work, toward a result you can both name. "Joining" is what you call it later, once that's in place.

Before you really start, three things should be clear:

1. **A project or research question you can actually name**, even if it's still rough.
2. **A mentor who has agreed to work with you.** It's good to approach people. A friendly conversation is not the same as a yes. And if nobody in your family has done this, you're still allowed to write. Nobody is born knowing how this works.
3. **An agreement on time, what you'll try to finish, and how long you're signing up for.** Hours per week. What "done" looks like. The end date.

You can talk before all three exist. You shouldn't treat the work as started until they do. Sitting in on a meeting, hanging around, even helping a little, does not automatically mean you're in the lab, that you get badge access, or that your name goes on a paper. Those things come from a real commitment. They don't come from proximity.

This sounds fussy. I think of it as the kind version. A vague arrangement wastes a semester and leaves both people with nothing they can stand behind.

## The first email

I don't have a separate post on this for undergraduates. The [PhD admissions](/blog/phd-admissions-reality) note about cold email is a different problem: applicants trying to get a faculty member's attention during admissions season. This is earlier. You're asking someone to spend time training you.

Don't send "I'd like to join the lab and do some work." Don't send a four-page generated proposal that name-drops every paper on the website. Both of those are common. Neither gives the other person anything to work with.

A first email needs four things:

- Who you are, in one line. Year, major, maybe a class you took.
- One thing you actually read, used, or got stuck on, and a question about it. The question can be naive. It cannot be fake.
- What you can actually give: hours per week, this semester or the summer.
- A CV.

You can write a PhD student in the group, not only the professor. Often that's the better first note.

A sample is a bad idea if you send it word for word. I will know, and so will anyone else who has read this. The point is the shape:

> Subject: Question about on-device inference in [paper]
>
> Hi Prof. Reddi — I'm a junior in EE. I took your embedded systems course last fall and then read [paper]. I don't fully follow how you handled [specific piece], and I've been wondering whether [a real question, even a small one].
>
> I have about 8–10 hours a week this semester. I'm trying to find out if this kind of work is for me, and I'm happy to start small. CV attached.

Change every specific. If you don't have a question yet, you're not ready to send the email. Read something first. One paper, or one lecture that bothered you, is enough.

## Know what you're trying to finish

If you want this to go well, you need a reasonably clear picture of what you're trying to do and what you want to get out of it.

It can be small. "By the end of the term I'll have this measurement pipeline producing honest numbers on these three devices." "I'll reproduce this result, write down where it breaks, and propose one experiment that would tell us why." Those are projects. "I want research experience" is a reason to start a conversation. It isn't a project.

Being specific forces a talk people skip: how much time do you actually have? A summer, or two years? Are you hoping for a paper, a letter, a skill, or a chance to see whether research is for you? All of those are fine. Leave them unsaid and you'll invent different stories about what success was supposed to be.

A paper is a nice outcome if it happens. **It isn't the point**, and not getting one doesn't mean the year was a failure. Often the more durable thing is a mentor who can actually speak for you.

I care a lot about ownership. You should have a piece of the work that's yours, not a role that amounts to watching, or loosely helping with someone else's project. "I helped" is a fine description of the first week. It shouldn't still be the description in month four.

## Pick one thing and do it well

Undergrad rewards a **flashlight**. You do a ton of things: clubs, leadership roles, classes, maybe a couple of research groups, a side project, a startup. That isn't wasted. It shows you can handle a lot, and it helps you look around. A flashlight is good for that. It lights up a whole room. It just doesn't go very far, and it doesn't cut anything.

Research, and whatever you want after it — a strong letter, an internship you actually get something from, a PhD application that isn't a collage — needs a **laser**. Same energy, much smaller spot. **Less is more.** The beam has to be narrow enough that it actually arrives.

I see a lot of students stay in flashlight mode because that's what worked. Two labs, three organizations, an independent study, plus something they mention in every email. It looks like ambition. From where I sit, it's often a year of dim light and nothing you'd call a result.

You still need enough range to notice when ideas connect. What people skip is the focus: pick one or two things you're willing to get good at, and stay there long enough to have some judgment, not just exposure. That's the laser.

Students often don't see this because nobody has told them how the next stage is evaluated. They assume that if they do ten things, they'll get ten great reviews. Usually the opposite happens. A letter that says you sampled a lot of rooms is a weak letter. A letter that says you stayed, you owned something, you got good at it, and you can be trusted with harder work — that letter actually does something.

## Letters matter more than you think

PhD admissions, internships, fellowships, and a surprising number of jobs are letter-driven in a way that's hard to see from inside an undergraduate program. [What a mentor can actually write](/blog/recommendation-letters) isn't a recap of your titles. It's a claim about your judgment, whether you follow through, how you act when you're stuck, and whether they would work with you again.

So you need someone who can honestly champion you. Not a famous name who barely knows you. A person who watched you do the work: stay with it, show up, recover from a bad week, finish what you said you'd finish. Depth gives them something true to say. Scatter leaves them writing a polite paragraph with no specifics.

If I could only tell students one thing, it would be this. **The scarce resource isn't a seat in a famous lab. It's a mentor who, when someone asks whether you'll be good to work with, can say yes without hedging.** You get that by doing one thing well, not by being nearby while a lot of things happen.

## Sound like yourself

Generative tools have changed the surface of all this. Anyone can produce a fluent email, a literature summary that sounds competent, or a project proposal that name-drops the right papers. I've seen students use these tools to write things they don't yet understand. I don't think that makes them dishonest. Wanting to sound like you belong is a very old undergraduate instinct. The tools just made it easier.

**Using a tool isn't the issue.** The issue is when the writing gets ahead of what you actually know, and then you have to spend the collaboration pretending. Mentors can tell. Sometimes in the first meeting. Sometimes when a simple follow-up question lands and there's nothing behind the sentence.

You don't need to show up as an expert. Curiosity, a willingness to learn, and showing up reliably matter more than already knowing the stack. The first weeks will feel slow. You're learning the papers, the tools, how the group thinks. That's normal. It doesn't mean you're behind. If you used a tool to get started, say what you actually understand and what you don't. If a paper is over your head, that's useful information, not a confession. Then go do the actual work: read until you can explain it, run the thing, look at the failure, change the setup, write down what happened.

A simple test: could you defend the claim without the chat history in front of you? If not, you don't know it yet. That's a fine place to be on Tuesday. It's a bad place to still be in week eight while presenting the claim as yours.

## Stay when it gets hard

Research is full of ambiguity and dead ends. That isn't a bug. That's most of the job.

The students who get something real out of a project are rarely the ones for whom everything worked on the first try. They're the ones who came back after the obvious approach failed, with some idea of why, instead of vanishing.

This is also why doing too many things at once is expensive. When the project gets hard, the other eight commitments become a respectable-looking way to leave. Depth needs fewer exits.

You'll have exams. You'll have a bad month. Tell your mentor. Being reliable doesn't mean you never struggle. It means you communicate, you show up, and you renegotiate in the open instead of fading out.

## Your graduate-student mentor is doing you a real favor

A lot of undergraduate research is mentored day to day by PhD students and postdocs, not by the faculty member whose name is on the door. Those people are putting real time into you. They are in the middle of their own training. An hour with you is an hour they didn't spend on their thesis.

Treat it as a two-way thing. They show you how the work is actually done. You owe them straightforward communication, consistent presence, and following through on what you said you'd do. If you can't make a meeting, say so. If you're lost, say so early. If you've decided this isn't for you, say that too, before they keep investing in a collaboration you've already left.

Faculty notice this even when they aren't in the room. It's one of the things that later shows up in letters, because it's one of the things that tells us whether someone will be a decent colleague.

## Use a summer to go deeper

Same idea applies to internships and research summers. Be honest about what the summer is for. A new environment can help. Another shallow tour usually doesn't.

If you already have a thread of work, use the summer to take it further, or to learn something that makes that thread stronger. If you don't have a thread yet, one serious project is still better than three introductions. The students who later have a real choice of internships, or a real shot at a research career, can usually describe a problem they stayed with. They don't recite a list of groups they passed through.

I wrote a [longer version of this for PhD students](/blog/internships-support-not-define). For undergraduates the direction is earlier and smaller, but the mistake is the same: taking whatever is available and hoping it will tell you what you care about.

If this stopped tomorrow, could a mentor tell a specific story about you — a time you used judgment, recovered, followed through? If they can only offer adjectives, you don't yet have the thing letters and later opportunities are actually built on. Stay until they can. One serious collaboration is enough.

**You don't have to know that you want a PhD.** Undergraduate research is a way to find out whether this kind of work is for you. That only works if you actually do the work.
